For a man who has spent a decade bending FIFA's statutes, its congress, and occasionally its own rulebook to his will, Gianni Infantino picked a fight this week that he could not win quickly enough to survive.

On Tuesday, the FIFA president unveiled a plan to carve out the organization's commercial engine — the World Cup, the Club World Cup, men's and women's tournaments alike — into a standalone company worth an estimated $20 billion. Roughly a fifth of it would go to outside investors, anchored by Thrive Eternal, the permanent holding vehicle run by venture capitalist Josh Kushner, brother of presidential son-in-law Jared Kushner. FIFA framed it as a way to unlock the true value of its broadcast and sponsorship rights. Member federations were dangled a $20 million signing bonus for their vote, with $66 million more promised down the line.

By Friday night, it was dead.

The collapse took less time than a World Cup group stage. What killed it wasn't a boardroom vote or a legal challenge — it was the sight of football's power blocs, normally too fractured to agree on kickoff times, suddenly speaking with one voice. UEFA came first, and came hardest, voting to boycott FIFA competitions altogether and accusing Infantino of trying to auction off the sport's soul in a deal hatched behind closed doors. CONCACAF followed. Then, more damagingly for Infantino personally, the Asian Football Confederation — a bloc that has been one of his most dependable allies for eleven years — joined the opposition too, warning that football should never have been dragged into this position in the first place.

The defections weren't limited to rival confederations. Carlos Cordeiro, a former Goldman Sachs banker serving as Infantino's own senior adviser and FIFA's representative on the White House's World Cup task force, resigned in protest, saying flatly that he wanted no part of a plan to sell off a stake in the tournament. Hours later came an even more pointed wound: FIFA's chief operating officer, Kevin Lamour, told reporters that staff had been kept in the dark and deserved better than the contempt they'd been shown — the kind of quote that doesn't leak from a healthy organization.

Even the Kushner connection, which had fueled speculation about a White House assist, turned out to be a liability rather than an asset. Donald Trump, appearing at Camp David, said he'd never discussed the proposal with Infantino at all — leaving the FIFA president without the political cover the deal's optics implied he might have.

By Friday evening, Infantino folded. In a statement, he said the project had created divisions that outweighed whatever backing it carried, and confirmed it would not go forward — insisting, in the same breath, that unity had always been the goal.

It was a startling admission from a man who has rarely, if ever, backed down from a fight of his own making.

**What happens now is the more interesting question.** Infantino is set to seek a final term through 2031, with member associations due to vote in Rabat, Morocco, and a candidacy deadline landing in mid-November. Before this week, he was expected to cruise in unopposed, buoyed by the goodwill of a smoothly delivered World Cup this past summer. That expectation now looks considerably shakier. A president who miscalculated this badly, this publicly, and lost his own inner circle in the process, is no longer an automatic coronation — even if nobody has yet stepped forward to challenge him directly.

For now, the World Cup remains fully in FIFA's hands. Whether Infantino remains in charge of FIFA is suddenly a live question again.

*Reporting based on statements from FIFA, UEFA, CONCACAF, and the Asian Football Confederation, and public remarks from Gianni Infantino, Carlos Cordeiro, Kevin Lamour, and Donald Trump.*
