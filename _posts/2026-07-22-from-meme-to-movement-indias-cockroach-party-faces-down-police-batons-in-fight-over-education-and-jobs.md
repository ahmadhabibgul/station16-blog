Two months ago, the Cockroach Janta Party didn't exist. Today it claims more Instagram followers than India's ruling Bharatiya Janata Party, has camped out for weeks at one of Delhi's main protest grounds, and just triggered the largest street clashes the capital has seen in years.

The movement's origin is almost accidental. On May 15, Chief Justice of India Surya Kant, addressing a Supreme Court case, described unemployed young people who criticize institutions online as "cockroaches" and "parasites of society." Within a day, Abhijeet Dipke — a 30-year-old political communications strategist who had previously worked with the Aam Aadmi Party — turned the insult into a rallying cry, launching a satirical "platform for all the cockroaches out there" with the tagline "Voice of the Lazy & Unemployed."

The name, a direct parody of the BJP itself, struck a nerve. Within days, more than 350,000 people had signed up and the group's Instagram following passed 20 million — eventually growing past 22 million, more than the official BJP account's roughly 9.4 million, despite the BJP being widely considered the world's largest political party by membership. The hashtag #MainBhiCockroach ("I too am a cockroach") spread rapidly across Indian social media.

**From joke to sit-in**

What started as memes and cockroach-costumed clean-up drives hardened into something more organized once anger turned toward India's exam system. The cancellation of a medical entrance exam and long-running allegations of leaks and irregularities in board and entrance tests became the movement's central grievance. By June, CJP supporters had set up an open-ended sit-in at Jantar Mantar, New Delhi's designated protest site, demanding the resignation of Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan.

The protest camp drew students, young professionals, and even families with children. It gained a further boost when veteran education activist Sonam Wangchuk joined in solidarity with a hunger strike — one that ended when authorities forcibly removed him from the site in mid-July.

**The march on Parliament**

Anger over Wangchuk's removal fed directly into the movement's biggest test yet: a "Chalo Sansad" ("March to Parliament") demonstration on July 20 that drew more than 10,000 people through central Delhi, defying police barricades and a march ban. Police responded with tear gas and baton charges. Delhi Police said roughly 180 people were hurt in the clashes — about 60 protesters and 118 security personnel — and that 70 demonstrators were detained. Videos circulating afterward showed officers striking protesters; no footage has surfaced showing violence initiated by marchers.

The following day, Dipke announced the group would pause its street marches, citing the safety of young participants, while vowing the broader protest would continue. Supporters have stayed put at Jantar Mantar under heavy police presence, insisting they won't leave until Pradhan resigns.

**A government caught off guard**

Modi's government, now in its third term, initially dismissed the movement, with BJP leadership accusing organizers of trying to "make the country hollow." But the scale of the July 20 march appears to have prompted a shift: officials arranged for two CJP representatives to meet Health Minister J.P. Nadda, though the education minister has so far refused to step down.

The party's online reach has also collided with government pressure — its X account has reportedly been made inaccessible within India at points, even as it remains visible abroad, raising concerns among opposition politicians about free expression.

CJP is not registered with the Election Commission of India and functions more as a protest movement than a conventional party, though its organizers say they intend to keep pushing on issues beyond education, including unemployment, corruption, and government transparency. Whether that translates into a lasting political force or fades once its immediate demands are met remains the open question hanging over Delhi's summer of dissent.
