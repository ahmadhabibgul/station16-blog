Pakistan has formally requested a $10 billion currency-stabilization facility from the United States, a rare form of Treasury support that would give the cash-strapped economy a fresh cushion for its foreign reserves, according to a source familiar with the matter.

The request, addressed to US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, seeks a Bilateral Exchange Stabilization Support Facility with a maturity of up to five years. If granted, it would mark one of the largest bilateral financial commitments the US has extended to Pakistan in years — and a notable departure from Islamabad's usual reliance on the International Monetary Fund.

## Leveraging a Diplomatic Moment

The timing is not incidental. Pakistan played a visible role in facilitating talks during the recent US-Iran war, a contribution that raised its profile in Washington and appears to have opened the door for Islamabad to press for economic concessions from the US and other partners.

Exchange stabilization facilities are an uncommon tool, typically administered through the US Treasury's Exchange Stabilization Fund, and used to supply dollars, currency swaps, or guarantees that help a country defend its reserves and steady its currency. Such arrangements carry weight beyond their dollar value — a green light from Washington would function as much as a political endorsement as a financial one.

## A Complement, Not a Replacement, for IMF Support

Pakistan remains in the middle of a $7 billion IMF program that has come with a steep political price: higher taxes, tighter spending, and structural reforms that have squeezed households and businesses alike. A US facility would not replace that program but would sit alongside it, easing pressure on the rupee and reducing Islamabad's dependence on IMF disbursements and one-off bilateral rescues from allies like Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and China.

Pakistan's central bank said earlier this year that it expects reserves to approach their 2021 peak, reaching roughly $20 billion by the end of 2026 — a recovery that a US backstop would accelerate and help protect.

Ratings agency Fitch noted in April that Pakistan's continued adherence to the IMF program has supported its overall funding capacity, while rebuilt foreign exchange buffers offer some insulation against external shocks, including those stemming from instability in the Middle East. The agency also cautioned that rising energy costs and potential supply disruptions could still erode those buffers.

## Still Unconfirmed

Neither government has publicly acknowledged the request. Pakistan's finance ministry did not respond to inquiries made outside Asia business hours, and the US Treasury declined to comment. Whether the facility is approved — and on what terms — remains an open question, with no indication yet of how quickly Washington might respond.
