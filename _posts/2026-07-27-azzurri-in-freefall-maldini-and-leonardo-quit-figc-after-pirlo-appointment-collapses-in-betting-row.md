There is a particular strain of chaos that only Italian football seems capable of manufacturing, and this week it delivered a vintage example. Paolo Maldini, brought in with fanfare on July 11 to rebuild a national team that has now missed three straight World Cups, is out. So is Leonardo, the trusted lieutenant he installed as his adviser. The reason, in the end, wasn't tactics or transfer budgets or even results. It was a betting company.

Andrea Pirlo — World Cup winner in 2006, Maldini's former teammate at Milan, and by last week the presumptive next head coach of the Azzurri — never got to sign his contract. His candidacy unraveled over a sponsorship deal struck last October, when Pirlo, then managing in the UAE, agreed to serve as global ambassador for Fonbet, one of Russia's largest sports-betting operators. In the abstract, a commercial endorsement. In the current Italian political climate, with the war in Ukraine still shaping foreign policy and public sentiment, it became something else entirely: a liability no federation president wanted attached to the national crest.

Pirlo didn't go quietly. He took to social media to insist the arrangement was strictly professional, that he had never expressed the political views being projected onto him, and that his attachment to Italy was not something a job title could diminish. It didn't matter. FIGC president Giovanni Malagò, only weeks into his own tenure after steering the Milan-Cortina Olympics, made the call to shut the appointment down — reportedly over the objections of the very men he'd empowered to make exactly this kind of decision.

That is the part that should worry Italian football far more than the coaching vacancy itself. Maldini and Leonardo weren't brought in as figureheads. They were handed the technical keys to a federation in crisis, tasked with identifying a manager after Gennaro Gattuso's exit and after both Pep Guardiola and Carlo Ancelotti had already said no. Pirlo was their answer — the candidate they judged best equipped to modernize a stagnant system and hand responsibility to a younger generation of players. When Malagò overruled them anyway, the message was unambiguous: authority in this new structure was symbolic, not real. Both men resigned rather than operate inside it.

The timing compounds the embarrassment. Sixteen days is barely enough to unpack an office, let alone reshape a footballing nation. Italy now finds itself back where it stood in April — without a head coach, without the technical leadership it had just installed to fix that exact problem, and with a candidate search that has already burned through its two most credible outside options in Guardiola and Ancelotti. Thiago Motta's name has reportedly been taken off the table entirely in the fallout. Roberto Mancini, who left this same federation under a cloud not so long ago, is once again the frontrunner. Antonio Conte, backed by voices inside Lega Serie A, lingers as the alternative. There's even talk of turning to Giorgio Chiellini to fill the technical director post Maldini has just vacated.

None of this fixes the underlying problem. Italy's football crisis was never really about a single appointment — it's structural, years in the making, visible in the youth pipeline and the domestic game long before it showed up as a playoff loss to Bosnia-Herzegovina. What this week made clear is that the federation's response to that crisis is still governed by boardroom politics rather than footballing judgment. Malagò got his veto. What he does with the credibility he spent to get it is the next question — and Italian football, again, is left waiting for someone else to answer it.
